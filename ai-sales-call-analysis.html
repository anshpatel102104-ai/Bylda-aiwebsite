AI Sales Call Analysis

What one call actually tells you.

A transcript is a record of words. Bylda reads a conversation the way an experienced manager does — the questions, the order, the pauses, who held control — and turns it into findings you can act on.

Transcription is not analysis

Accurate transcription has been a commodity for years. Every vendor has roughly the same word error rate, and none of it answers a manager's question, because the interesting information in a sales call is not the words — it is the structure.

Who asked the questions. How many, and how open. Whether discovery happened before the pitch or after it. How long the rep waited before answering an objection, and how long they talked once they started. Which moment the buyer stopped asking questions. Whether the person who signs was even in the room.

Two calls can contain nearly identical words and mean opposite things. The difference is in the order and the timing.

From conversation to finding

Bylda works in stages, and the vocabulary is precise because the stages are distinct.

01 Trace Raw behavioral evidence, timestamped. 09:12 — price stated. No decision questions asked before this point.
02 Signal The traces that mattered to this conversation. Buyer asked six questions before pricing, none after.
03 Echo Whether this call repeats a wider pattern. Same sequence in half of this rep's recent conversations.
04 Directive The change worth making next time. Ask how they decide before you quote.
05 Outcome What followed — the test of all of the above. No second meeting booked.

Boundaries

What Bylda does not claim to read.

A lot of call-analysis marketing implies the software knows how people felt. We are going to be plain about this, because it affects whether you should trust anything else on this site.

Bylda reports observable conduct: what was said, by whom, in what order, how long after, how often. Those are facts about a recording, and you can check every one of them.

Bylda does not score emotion, infer confidence as an internal state, type personalities, or tell you a buyer was excited. Inferring feelings from a voice is contested science, it produces numbers that look precise and are not, and in workplace settings it is increasingly restricted by regulation. A product that measures conduct is more useful anyway — because conduct is the part a rep can actually change.

FAQ

Sales call analysis, answered.

Start with structure rather than words: count and classify the questions each side asked, measure how long discovery ran and what it covered, note where pricing landed relative to decision criteria, measure how quickly the rep responded to each objection and how long they then talked, check who actually attended, and mark the point where buyer engagement changed. Those observations explain outcomes; a word-level transcript does not.

Three things first. Whether discovery preceded the pitch. Whether the person who signs was ever present. And whether the stated loss reason matches the conversation — "lost on price" very often turns out to be a deal where price arrived before value or criteria were established, which is a sequencing problem rather than a pricing one.

Keyword search tells you a topic came up. It cannot tell you the topic came up in the wrong order, or that the response to it doubled the rep's talk time. The information in a sales call is largely structural — sequence, timing, distribution — and that is what Bylda measures.

Not as claims about internal states. Bylda measures observable delivery — speaking pace, pauses, interruptions, response latency — because those are verifiable facts about a recording. It does not score how someone felt. Emotion inference from voice is contested science and, in workplace contexts, increasingly restricted by regulation.
