The Category

Commercial behavior intelligence.

A CRM records what sales teams say happened. Commercial behavior intelligence learns what actually happened, why it happened, and what the team should do next.

The definition

Commercial behavior intelligence is software that observes how deals actually unfold — the conversations, objections, stakeholder changes, engagement shifts, and follow-up patterns between buyers and sellers — and turns that understanding into concrete action: an updated CRM, a flagged risk, a drafted follow-up, a recommended next move.

The distinction from everything that came before is the unit of analysis. Sales software has been organized around records for thirty years: account, contact, opportunity, stage, amount. But revenue does not happen because a field changes. It happens through behavior.

A deal is not a row in a database. It is a sequence of human behavior that a database was never designed to hold.

What behavior looks like

Every one of these events moves a real deal, and none of them fits in a CRM field:

  • A buyer raises a pricing concern — and the seller responds with a discount instead of resolving the actual objection.
  • A champion who replied same-day for a month stops responding.
  • Procurement enters the deal earlier than usual.
  • The economic buyer has attended zero of six calls.
  • A competitor gets mentioned for the first time, late in the cycle.
  • The decision timeline slips — softly, in a sentence nobody logged.

Teams that can see these sequences act earlier and more precisely. Teams that can't find out at the end of the quarter, when the pipeline report finally catches up with reality.

Why now

The reason sales software stopped at records was practical: behavior was unobservable. Nobody could parse ten thousand hours of conversation, correlate it with email cadence and CRM state, and keep it current. So the industry asked salespeople to summarize their own behavior into fields — and thirty years of stale pipelines, Friday-afternoon data entry, and forecast surprises followed.

That constraint is gone. Language models can now read a conversation the way a good sales manager does — the concern behind the question, the commitment that wasn't quite made — and do it for every interaction, every day. The record-keeping becomes a byproduct. The understanding becomes the product.
