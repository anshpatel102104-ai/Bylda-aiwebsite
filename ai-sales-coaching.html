AI Sales Coaching

Coaching that names one thing.

Most sales coaching fails for a boring reason: it arrives late, it is based on whichever call a manager happened to hear, and it asks a rep to change five things at once. Bylda gives one directive, from the rep's own conversations, with the moment that justifies it.

Why generic AI coaching does not change behavior

Point a language model at a transcript and it will produce plausible advice: ask more open questions, build rapport, confirm next steps. All true, all useless — because the rep already knows it, it is not specific to what they did, and there is no evidence attached that would make them believe it applies to them.

Coaching changes behavior when three things are true at once. It is specific enough to execute on the next call. It is grounded in something the rep actually did, that they can go and verify. And there is exactly one of it.

A rep given five things to fix will fix none of them. That is not a discipline problem, it is a working-memory problem.

What a Directive is

Bylda's coaching output is a Directive: the single change most likely to matter, expressed as an action rather than a quality.

  • Not "improve your objection handling." Instead: "Pause two seconds after a pricing objection, then ask one question before you answer."
  • Not "do better discovery." Instead: "Establish how they decide, and who signs, before you quote a number."
  • Not "build more urgency." Instead: "Confirm the signer will attend before you schedule the next call."

Each one is derived from an Echo — a behavior that has repeated across that rep's conversations — not from a single call, and not from a generic playbook.

The part that matters

A directive that stops working stops being given.

This is the difference between coaching software and a coaching opinion. Because Bylda sees the behavior, the change, and the outcome that followed, it can tell whether the advice it gave was any good.
