CRM adoption was never a training problem

Ask any revenue leader what percentage of their CRM is accurate and you will get a rueful laugh, followed by a number somewhere between thirty and fifty. Ask what they have tried, and you will get the same list every time: required fields, weekly hygiene blocks, dashboards that shame the laggards, spiffs, enablement sessions, and eventually, a stern meeting.

None of it works for long. The number always drifts back down. After two decades of this, the honest conclusion is that we have been treating a structural problem as a motivational one.

The math nobody says out loud

A rep with thirty active opportunities is being asked to recall, structure, and type the substance of roughly fifteen conversations a week. Each one has a next step, a shifting close date, a stakeholder who was mentioned once, and an objection that changes the deal's shape.

Doing that well takes about six hours. Doing it badly takes forty minutes. The rep is compensated on closed revenue, not on data quality, and both versions look identical to the CRM.

We built a system that requires perfect recall from people we pay to be persuasive, and then we acted surprised.

Why required fields make it worse

The standard response to bad data is to force more of it. This reliably backfires, because a required field does not produce accurate data — it produces a filled field. Ask a rep to select a close date to save the record and you will get a close date. It will be the end of the quarter, and it will be wrong.

Every hygiene mandate has this shape. It converts missing data, which is honestly useless, into fabricated data, which is dangerously useful-looking. Forecasts built on the second are worse than forecasts built on the first, because nobody distrusts them.

The only fix that has ever held

Remove the human from the data path. Not from the relationship, not from the judgment, not from the negotiation — from the transcription.

When the CRM is written from what was actually said, three things change at once. Completeness stops being a discipline metric and becomes a technical guarantee. The data reflects the conversation rather than the rep's end-of-week mood. And managers stop spending their one-on-ones as data-entry audits.

When the record is written from what was actually said rather than recalled at the end of the week, completeness stops depending on anyone's discipline — because nobody is being asked for discipline. The right way to judge the size of that effect is against your own baseline, not against a figure from someone else's deployment.
