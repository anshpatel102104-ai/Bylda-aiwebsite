Conversation intelligence is not the product

The first generation of conversation intelligence did something genuinely new: it made sales calls searchable. Before that, a call happened, two people remembered it differently, and the CRM got a sentence.

That was a real advance. It is also, at this point, table stakes — the underlying models are available to anyone, and accuracy differences between vendors have collapsed to rounding errors.

The insight-to-action gap

Here is what nobody puts on the pricing page: an insight that requires a human to act on it has the same adoption problem as the CRM field it was supposed to replace.

A platform flags that a competitor was mentioned on Tuesday. Excellent. Now someone has to notice the flag, decide it matters, open the deal record, update the competitive field, adjust the strategy, and tell the manager. Each of those steps has a completion rate below one. Multiply them together and you get the actual value delivered, which is a fraction of what was promised in the demo.

Most conversation intelligence deployments produce beautiful analysis of calls that changed nothing.

Coaching is not the same as executing

The strongest defense of the category is coaching, and it is a fair one — reviewing real calls does make reps better. But coaching is a slow, human, deliberately-paced activity. It cannot be the primary return on a platform that touches every conversation your company has.

Meanwhile the fast, mechanical, high-volume work sitting right next to the insight — writing the field, drafting the follow-up, updating the forecast, scheduling the next meeting — gets handed back to the rep, who is the slowest and most expensive way to do it.

The right question to ask a vendor

Not "how accurate is your transcription?" Everyone's is fine. Ask instead:

  • When your system detects a new decision maker, what changes in my CRM — and how fast?
  • When a deal goes quiet, who finds out, and is the outreach already written?
  • If my reps never open your interface, what still happens?

The third question is the one that separates a recorder from a system that actually operates. A tool that produces nothing when unobserved is a tool your team will eventually stop observing.
